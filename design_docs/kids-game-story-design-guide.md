# Kids Game Story Design Guide

Use this guide when designing, reviewing, or generating story content for a game aimed at primary-school children.

The story's job is not only to entertain. It should help the child understand:

- **What am I trying to do?**
- **Why should I care?**
- **What happens when I make progress?**

---

## 1. Give the Player One Crystal-Clear Goal

The child should be able to explain the main objective in one simple sentence.

### Good examples

- Find the five missing magical lanterns.
- Help the village restore its colours.
- Rescue the lost animals and bring them home.
- Repair the broken skyship before the festival begins.

### Avoid

- Too many simultaneous objectives.
- Heavy lore before the player understands the basic mission.
- Complicated political conflicts or long explanations.
- Goals that are only understandable after several cutscenes.

### Agent check

Ask:

> Can a primary-school child explain the main goal in one sentence?

If not, simplify it.

---

## 2. Make the Player the Hero

The story should move forward because of what the player does.

The child should not feel like a spectator watching NPCs solve the important problems.

### Good player actions

- Solving puzzles.
- Exploring new areas.
- Finding missing objects.
- Helping characters.
- Repairing things.
- Making simple choices.
- Collecting items that visibly change the world.

### Good story response

When the player succeeds, something should happen:

- A bridge is repaired.
- A new area opens.
- A character becomes happier.
- A village becomes brighter.
- A new ability is unlocked.
- The environment visibly changes.

### Agent check

Ask:

> Did the story progress because of the player's action?

If the answer is mostly "no," give the player more agency.

---

## 3. Keep Emotional Stakes Exciting but Safe

Children can enjoy danger, mystery, suspense, and scary moments, but the tone should remain age-appropriate.

A useful pattern is:

**Tension → Player Action → Resolution → Reassurance**

### Suitable stakes

- A storm damaged the village.
- A creature stole an important object.
- The forest lost its colours.
- A character is temporarily lost.
- A festival may not happen unless the player helps.

### Use extra care with

- Permanent death.
- Abandonment.
- Graphic violence.
- Intense horror.
- Cruel punishment.
- Hopeless situations.
- Long periods of unresolved fear.

The goal is not to remove conflict. Conflict makes stories interesting.

The goal is to make sure the child feels:

> "This problem is serious, but I can help fix it."

### Agent check

Ask:

> Does the conflict create motivation without creating prolonged fear or distress?

---

## 4. Create Memorable Characters With Clear Personalities

Important characters should be easy to understand and remember.

For younger players, start with a strong, recognizable personality before adding complexity.

### Simple character formula

Give each important character:

1. **One dominant personality trait**
2. **One recognizable visual feature**
3. **One role in the player's adventure**

### Example

**Pip the Fox**

- Personality: Brave but clumsy
- Visual identity: Oversized explorer backpack
- Role: Helps the player discover hidden paths

**Momo the Dragon**

- Personality: Sleepy and forgetful
- Visual identity: Tiny glasses
- Role: Gives the player riddles and clues

### Avoid

- Too many named characters introduced at once.
- Characters with nearly identical personalities.
- Long backstories before the player cares about them.
- Dialogue that exists only to explain lore.

### Agent check

Ask:

> Can the player describe this character using one or two memorable traits?

---

## 5. Make Story Progression Match Gameplay Progression

The story and gameplay should feel like the same adventure.

Avoid creating a story that claims something is extremely urgent while the gameplay encourages unrelated activities for long periods.

### Good progression

**Solve puzzles → Repair bridge → Enter new area → Meet new character → Unlock ability**

The player's gameplay achievement should produce a story consequence.

### Example

The player completes three maths challenges.

Instead of only showing:

> "Level Complete!"

Use the success to change the world:

> The windmill starts turning again, powering the lift to the mountain village.

Now the educational activity, gameplay progression, and story progression all support each other.

### Agent check

Ask:

> After completing this gameplay objective, what changes in the story or world?

If the answer is "nothing," consider adding a visible consequence.

---

# Recommended Story Loop

A simple story structure that works well for children's games:

**Goal → Challenge → Small Win → World Changes → Bigger Challenge → Celebration**

Example:

1. The Moonlight Festival is tonight.
2. Five magical lanterns have gone missing.
3. The player explores different areas to find them.
4. Each recovered lantern brings part of the town back to life.
5. New characters and challenges appear.
6. Returning the final lantern starts the festival.

This structure allows the game to feel rewarding even before the final ending.

---

# Dialogue Guidelines

Keep dialogue:

- Short.
- Clear.
- Warm.
- Easy to read aloud.
- Focused on the player's next action.

Prefer:

> "The bridge is broken! Can you find three wooden planks?"

Over:

> "According to the ancient builders, this bridge once connected the eastern settlement with the neighbouring woodland province..."

Use deeper lore as optional material rather than required instructions.

---

# Visual Storytelling Rule

Whenever possible, show story progress visually instead of explaining everything through text.

Examples:

- Flowers bloom after an area is restored.
- Lights turn on in rescued buildings.
- NPCs return to the town square.
- Clouds disappear.
- Broken machines begin moving.
- Music becomes richer as the world recovers.

This is especially helpful for younger children and children with different reading abilities.

---

# The No-Dialogue Test

A powerful final check:

> If all dialogue were removed, would the child still roughly understand what they are supposed to do?

They should be able to understand the basic objective through:

- Character expressions.
- Animation.
- Environment design.
- Icons.
- Object placement.
- Map markers.
- Visual changes.

Dialogue should strengthen understanding, not carry the entire game.

---

# Agent Review Checklist

When creating or reviewing a story, verify the following:

- [ ] The main goal can be explained in one sentence.
- [ ] The player causes important story events.
- [ ] Challenges feel exciting without being excessively frightening.
- [ ] Important characters have clear and memorable personalities.
- [ ] Gameplay progress causes visible story progress.
- [ ] Dialogue is short and easy to understand.
- [ ] The game avoids unnecessary lore dumps.
- [ ] The player regularly receives small wins.
- [ ] The world reacts visibly to the player's actions.
- [ ] The objective remains understandable without relying entirely on dialogue.
- [ ] The ending provides a clear sense of achievement and celebration.

---

# Agent Priority Order

When trade-offs are necessary, prioritize:

1. **Clarity**
2. **Player agency**
3. **Emotional safety**
4. **Memorable characters**
5. **Story-gameplay connection**
6. Additional lore and world-building

A simple story that children understand and enjoy interacting with is better than a sophisticated story they cannot follow.

---

# Core Principle

The player should consistently feel:

> **"I know what I need to do, I can make a difference, and I want to see what happens next."**

If the story supports those three feelings, it is probably doing its job.
